01 Introduction

Application for Senior Product Designer, App Experience

Dear Hiring Team at Zoe,

I'm writing this cover letter without AI, for a change, plus an AI-assisted attachment below.

I've been a long fan of the company and the science that powers the Zoe ethos. I have myself as a woman, struggled in life with cultivating a positive relationship with food so I can be healthier, have more energy and feel better. It's a struggle that so many women know all too well (men too, of course, but diet culture and nutrition marketing are overwhelmingly aimed at women) and while I haven't achieved yet the level of perfect controlled dieting (I love food too much), I'm always keenly aware of how important food and especially, gut health is to our bodies and minds.

That's why when the opportunity was presented I excitedly took part in ZOE's Women in Tech Hackathon in July. My team designed and built an AI-powered misinformation decoder for wellness, beauty and supplement content, end to end, in 48 hours. The hard part wasn't the AI which I used happily, but it was working out how to show someone how confident the evidence actually is behind a health claim, without either overwhelming them or falsely reassuring them. I've really enjoyed ideating, thinking and working in this problem space and the Zoe hackathon was fantastic in making space for these problems.

This particular problem, of making complex scientific information land with someone making a real decision about their own body, perhaps even when they're in a heightened emotional state and vulnerable to rash, quick decisions, is the one I find most interesting in this field. Because I am one of those people, and I need to design for people like me. I am the end user I tend to design for. Figuring out how data can be presented in a snapshot or easy to digest facts rather than clinical jargon is a craft problem as much as an information design one, and it's where I want to be spending my time.

Right now, I have 15 years designing end-to-end web and mobile products across healthtech, fintech, e-commerce and hardware-connected devices. Four of those years were as the only designer on a regulated web fintech product taken from zero, where I owned the brand, the information architecture, the design system and every screen. I also designed many consumer-facing mobile apps throughout my career, mostly in healthcare and femtech. Most recently in healthtech, I designed conditional onboarding and personalised dashboard states for people navigating fertility, menopause and neurodiversity, work that was fundamentally about deciding what to surface, when, and on what basis.

I'm applying to this Senior Product Designer role at Zoe for a few reasons. The first is that this is not a role for someone who only wants to optimise. I've done that and can do that kind of work, experiment-driven work based on hypothesis, and I'm comfortable with evidence, but the work I'm proudest of is where the problem wasn't defined yet and I had to generate genuinely different directions before choosing one. I'm a creative, deep thinker, empath and obsessive solutions finder and artist at heart. Finding solutions to problems, whether I'm working on a product or fixing my life, is one of my strongest, most human, most resilient and proudest strengths. I apply that skill generously to all my design work.

The second reason is that Zoe takes a scientific approach while still asking for strong design convictions. I love that. I love nerding out on the science, on the design and I love learning and I love changing my mind based on new evidence available, whilst always being true to my values. This is the kind of environment I want to work in.

I took some time to reflect below how I meet each of the points under What You'll Bring, with the specific work behind each one.

Best regards,

Irina Csapo

02 What I'll Bring

The points under What You'll Bring, and the work behind each one

Strong end-to-end consumer native app work, talked about honestly

Veva is a native iOS and Android app controlling home EV charge points. The charger has no screen, so the app was the entire interface to an unpredictable physical object. I designed the full state model: connecting, charging, paused, scheduled, error, offline, and every transition between them, so it stayed legible on a phone even when the hardware itself was behaving unreliably.

A decision I'd defend: two very different users, installing engineers and homeowners, in one app rather than two. I rejected separate builds on maintenance cost and because the engineer is frequently setting the charger up with the homeowner present. That created a real trade-off, a settings area complete enough for an engineer and safe enough for a homeowner to stumble into, which is why I've designed a qualified-electrician warning gate.

What I'd do differently: I accepted the brief's success criteria rather than defining my own. I have no post-launch data on whether the guided setup actually reduced support calls, which was the entire point of that work. I'd now insist on the measure before starting.

Case study here → irinacsapo.co.uk/case-studies/veva-case-study

Genuinely creative, real range rather than variations on one idea

At Fertifa I designed onboarding where none existed. I produced three genuinely different directions rather than three versions of the same one:

  • A comprehensive, employer-configured flow that surfaced everything relevant to that employer's package.

  • A short progressive flow that personalised as it went, asking less and inferring more.

  • A minimal version that got people into the product immediately and deferred everything else to the point of need.

Each was a different bet about what a first-time user actually needs, with a different cost in build complexity and maintenance. Presenting them as real alternatives rather than a preferred option with two strawmen made the conversation about the bet rather than the aesthetics.

Energised by ambiguity, comfortable questioning the idea I was handed

At Fertifa, a free-text box was proposed for the end of onboarding. I argued against it.

Free text is work at precisely the moment you're trying to reduce work, and it assumes an articulacy people don't have yet. Someone knows they're dealing with menopause. They don't yet know their question. Asking them to compose one is asking them to do the hardest part first.

I proposed replacing it with example questions, which solve the blank page problem and also do quiet permission work, seeing a question written down tells someone their concern counts, plus a direct route to booking in a chosen area of care. The free text moves to the point of booking an appointment, where someone has already committed and has a genuine reason to be specific. I decided to relocate the text box to where it earns its place.

Experience designing for habit and behaviour change

Strands is a behavioural app for people living with trichotillomania (the urge of hair-pulling), built around daily check-ins, trigger identification and pattern recognition over time. The central design problem was showing someone their own behaviour without it reading as judgement, which is close to what you describe about scores and trends feeling encouraging rather than clinical.

Specific decisions: streaks framed around consistency of noticing rather than abstinence, so a difficult day doesn't reset progress. Insights written as observations rather than verdicts. Pacing that lets someone log in five seconds on a bad day and go deeper on a good one.

On the evidence: I had access to one person with the condition, so I treated her as a collaborator rather than a sample. That gave me real depth on one person's experience, enough to shape the emotional design, the language and the pacing. It was not enough to tell me about variation between people or which features mattered most, and I was explicit with myself about that line. Next would have been comparative sessions and a concept test on whether the streak mechanic motivates or shames this particular population.

WeGlow is a fitness app where I designed entire journeys, flows and medals for rewarding commitment and creating user habit and behaviour change.

Thinking in user problems, flows and behaviours rather than screens

Fertifa was fundamentally a systems problem. Pathways/services varied by employer package tier, so the same landing experience was wrong for almost everyone. The design work was defining the conditional logic, deciding what to hide, when, and on what basis, and building a personalised dashboard whose content states derived from onboarding responses and package tier. The screens were the output. The system underneath was the actual work.

Strong product judgement, balancing user needs, business goals, evidence and craft

At Omaze I redesigned the subscription cancellation flow. The commercial goal was retention, but adding friction to cancellation would have damaged trust and increased chargebacks, trading a short-term number for a long-term cost. I surfaced relevant alternatives at the point of intent instead. Cancellations fell 9.5% in three months without anyone being made to feel trapped.

A second example of evidence changing my mind: I was asked to make an add-on step more visually prominent. The funnel data showed drop-off happening before the display point, so prominence couldn't have been the constraint. I reframed it as value framing and sequencing and tested that instead, which increased attachment rate by 27%. Honestly, the data told me where, not why. The why was a hypothesis I tested rather than a finding, and I'd now run a handful of user sessions alongside the quant to confirm it.

Communicating with clarity and bringing people with me

I've spent some of my career as the only designer in the room, which means work only happens if I can explain it. I've presented design rationale to senior stakeholders to secure buy-in for platform redesigns, mentored junior designers, and this year gave a public talk at Milton Keynes Geek Night, Tender Tech: Joy, E-Waste and the Art of Delightful Making: mkgeeknight.co.uk/events/mkgn-56

Taking work from an ambiguous starting point through to shipped

Three years as the sole designer on Paycada, a B2B fintech payments product taken from zero in a regulated environment. Brand, information architecture, interaction patterns, component library, compliance constraints, every screen. No other designer to hand off to which meant building my own feedback loops, primarily with engineering: irinacsapo.co.uk/case-studies/paycada-case-study

Alongside client work I design and ship AI-powered products independently at irina.love. Four are live. I own the product thinking, the interface, the prompt design and enough of the front-end build to ship and maintain them myself.

Low-ego, pragmatic, ownership through to the end

Working with the lead engineer at Fertifa on the conditional entitlement logic, several of my proposals didn't quite make it through. Rather than either of us defending a first idea, we'd find the actual constraint and design around it. The result was better than what I started with, and that's usually how it goes.

I also stay in the work through implementation and QA rather than handing over a file and moving on. On Veva that meant producing the charging state animations in Rive and converting them to JSON when the developers needed specific export paths.

Using AI where it genuinely helps, and knowing where it doesn't

I design and build my own AI products, so I know where these systems are capable and, more usefully, where they break in front of real users.

Where it helps

AI has moved the bottleneck in my process. Generating options was never the hard part. Specification, documentation and audit were, because they cost days and get cut first under deadline pressure. Those now cost hours. I recently reverse-engineered three years of undocumented design system work into a three-tier token architecture, brand primitives, semantic aliases and component tokens, and ran a WCAG contrast audit alongside it that surfaced two live accessibility failures with a single-token fix for each. That would have been a week. It took an evening.

Where it doesn't

Judgement about what's worth building, and anything presented as fact. During that same exercise the model confidently produced hex values for colours that had never existed in the brand system. Plausible, internally consistent, entirely invented. I caught it by checking against source rather than against the rendered output. AI fails most dangerously where it's most fluent, so I verify anything factual and treat anything generative as a draft.

04 About me

Shipping growth, conversion, engagement. Maker of delightful things

Product Designer with 15+ years shipping growth-focused web and mobile experiences across healthtech, e-commerce, and fintech. I translate complex metrics, business goals, insights and user needs into conversion flows, onboarding systems, and retention experiments that perform, and at Omaze, my designs contributed to £1.5m revenue uplift and measurable improvements in CVR and churn. I work embedded with engineering, CRO, and growth teams from brief through handoff, designing testable solutions grounded in data.

Outside corporate work, I build irina.love, a collection of AI-powered apps exploring technology, emotion, and delight. I'm also a mixed-media artist working with e-waste and collage. Both practices share the same impulse, to create things people love.
